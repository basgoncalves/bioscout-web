What these numbers are, and are not

BioScout Web · the long version of every "model estimate" line in the app · back to the app

The one-sentence version

BioScout measures kinematics — where your joints were, and when — from phone video. Everything else on the results page (muscle forces, joint moments, joint contact forces, ground reaction) is computed or predicted from those kinematics plus your body mass. None of it is a force measurement. There is no force plate, no EMG, and no motion-capture rig.

This page exists so the notes in the app can stay short. Nothing here is a disclaimer in the legal sense — it is the list of things that would make a number wrong, written by someone who would rather you knew.

Muscle and joint contact forces

These come from a neural-network surrogate of OpenSim static optimisation, trained on running and single-leg squats. On subjects it never saw, the predicted curve tracks the real one with a median within-trial R² of about 0.42 for muscle force and 0.79 for joint contact force. The figures shown in the app are the model's own, read from the force model it loaded, so they are the ones that apply to your session rather than these.

Within-trial R² is the honest number and the lower one. A pooled R² is computed across every held-out frame at once, so it is rewarded for telling a sprint from a squat — which is not the question anyone reading this panel is asking.

Two limits that no amount of data fixes

  • It cannot know external load. The input is kinematics. An empty bar and a loaded bar at the same depth, at the same speed, produce the same prediction. If you train with added load, the absolute newtons are wrong — the app still asks for the load because everything else on the page uses it, but the surrogate does not.
  • Every output is a lower-limb muscle. A pull-up, a dip or a push-up is outside its domain entirely. The app will still draw curves, because the model returns numbers for anything you hand it; those numbers are not about your arms.

Squats are a short extrapolation from single-leg squats and are the best-supported case after running. In every case, treat the ranking — which muscle is working hardest, which joint is loaded most, how that changes between your own sets — as more reliable than the absolute value.

The joint-load dials

Each arrow is the predicted joint contact force at one instant: length is the force in bodyweights on a rim shared by every dial, direction is its angle in the chosen anatomical plane. The last column traces the arrow tip over the whole cycle (dot = start, cross = end). Hip is the load on the socket, in the pelvis frame; knee on the tibia; ankle on the talus. The bone outlines are schematic drawings, not your anatomy.

The frontal view is the weakest thing on the page. The side-to-side component is inferred from side-on motion alone — hip adduction and rotation are not visible to a single camera — so it shows the training group's typical frontal loading at your sagittal state, not yours. Read it as a pattern. It is not a knee-valgus measure and not an injury-risk measure, and using it as one would be inventing a finding.

The Reference ring, when a task has one, is a magnitude and not a direction: it says how large the force gets in the literature, not which way it pointed. Most movements have no reference at all — the corpus holds squats, the gait literature holds walking and running — and the app says so in red rather than borrowing a squat reference for a different task.

Joint moments and the ground reaction

Moments are inverse dynamics from your body mass, and the ground reaction is derived from whole-body centre-of-mass acceleration rather than assumed. That is a real calculation, not a prediction — but it rests on assumptions:

  • Centre of pressure is taken as the midfoot.
  • The two legs are assumed symmetric. A visible lean breaks that, and the app cannot tell.
  • A small flexor knee moment in a shallow squat is physical, not an error.

For hanging and pressing movements, the hands carry the whole body and the feet nothing, so the force at the hands is derived the same way and split equally between the two arms. Forearm and hand are one segment and the grip is taken at the wrist. Each arm is solved in the plane the camera sees; an oblique camera mixes the two planes and understates both.

For a jump shot, the ball is taken as 0.6 kg, carried by the shooting hand until release and nothing after. The guide hand's share is ignored, so the moment before release is slightly overstated. Release is the peak-wrist-height proxy used everywhere else in the report.

Muscle contributions to a moment

Where the app shows contributions, they are predicted muscle force times the moment arm read from the OpenSim model at that joint angle. The arms are the model's; the forces are the surrogate's and carry its errors. Read them as a ranking of who is doing the work, not as newton-metres you could publish.

They will not sum to the net moment, and are not meant to: only the muscles crossing the joint are drawn, co-contraction is not resolved, and the two legs are lumped. Where moment arms are not available, the app ranks the muscles that cross the joint by predicted peak force instead, which is a different and weaker statement.

The force-time integral

The ranked bars are ∫|F|dt over the rep, in newton-seconds, both sides summed. This is not work, and is not called work anywhere in the app: work needs the fibre's velocity, and the surrogate predicts force only. It answers "which muscle did the most over the whole rep" rather than "at one instant", and it inherits every limitation of the forces above.

Reference data

Reference columns and curves are not measurements of you. The squat reference is this project's own CEINMS run; walking and running peaks come from the gait literature. Walking and running are different tasks from whatever you recorded — they are there to answer one question, which is whether a predicted force is even the right size.

Kinematics, which is the part that is measured

Angles come from pose landmarks and are clipped to the OpenSim model's range. They are sagittal measurements and are only valid filmed from the side; the app warns you when a clip looks frontal. An ankle angle cannot be measured when the foot points toward or away from the camera, and is written as 0 in the export rather than as a fabricated value.

Timing resolution is the frame rate. A clip filmed at 30 fps locates take-off and landing to ±33 ms, and jump height goes as the square of flight time, so a low frame rate degrades it faster than it looks. This is the one limitation you can fix yourself: film at 60 fps or more.

Not a medical device

BioScout does not diagnose, treat or give medical advice, and nothing on the results page is a clinical measurement. If something hurts, ask a professional — not a surrogate model of a simulation of someone else's squat.
